1. The question, and where his own files already answer it

The question of 12 November: push another ball right up to one, let them connect at certain levels but always leave a layer of gap; these should be the angles and connections of a crystal lattice. On 14 November: there is usually some overlap on an outer or inner layer, for the push and the pull, the holding and the excluding.

In his files a second ball is not a second sphere. It is the next node of the tree, one edge of length ln z away, and the tree looks the same from every node (One Tree One Ball). Each ball then owns the region nearer to it than to any neighbour. Its walls are the planes halfway to each neighbour, at ln z / 2 along each arm. These are the planes the paper uses in Theorem 8.4, and the Commutator Eye draws the same construction as caps.

So in the one ball, with six arms: inside the wall the neighbours overlap, and the tree is a crystal exactly when a whole number n of balls fit round each edge, cos(360°/n) = q. Between those steps the balls never settle (free, not discrete). At the wall the walls touch at the rim. Past it there is a gap layer, nothing closes and every word is free. One number q = R/T of the step carries all of it; BallJoins J4 is the same q.

Recognised, not looked up today: the n = 5 and n = 6 cases are the two cubic honeycombs of hyperbolic space (five and six cubes round an edge; the second has its corners on the rim). What is his is reaching them from the peel step: the six-arm tree of ratio z is that honeycomb exactly at z = 2.8901, where cosh ln z is the golden ratio, and at z = 2 + √3, where Γ² = 1/3. The whole-number steps 5 and 7 are not closing steps: 5 sits between n = 8 and n = 10 (α = 36.87°), 7 is past the wall.

4. What this does to the balls mixer v1

  • Its scene 5 repeats the Commutator Eye with less in it (no caps, no residues, no doubled steps), and counts the turn the long way round.
  • Its scenes 2 to 4 use separate spheres in flat space. The gap there, 2/(zⁿ + 1), is the coordinate distance from a cut level to the rim. It is exact, but it is not the layer between neighbours; that layer is δ.
  • Its lattice scene (simple, body-centred and face-centred cubic from 6, 8, 12 arms) is the flat limit z → 1 of the one-ball picture, where α → 90°. In the ball itself the six-arm tree closes only at the steps of section 2.
  • Nothing in v1 is wrong as arithmetic. It answers the question in a frame that is not the one his papers use.

5. To talk about

  1. Which frame is the crystal in. One ball with overlap angle α and gap δ (his papers), or separate spheres (v1). The first gives closing steps that are not whole numbers; the second gives the ordinary cubic lattices but only as the flat limit.
  2. What n means for matter. n balls round an edge: 4 is flat space, 5 and 6 are the first two curved crystals, the wall is n → ∞. Whether an element's step picks an n is open; z = 3 (the 2/3 line of the space step) gives α = 70.53°, the angle whose cosine is 1/3, and is not a closing step.
  3. Radiating and receiving. The bridge page's two handed weights are a pair from his own work that changes sign when flipped. That is a better candidate than the bare sign of Γ used in v1. Untested.
  4. Eight and twelve arms. Everything in section 2 is the six-arm tree. The eight-way form at 7 and the face-diagonal set have other wall angles (arms at 70.53° and 60°); not run.
  5. The knot side. The same square and the same line decide when a knot's two meridians turn (Theorem 8.6). Whether a knot's commutator angle lands on 360°/n at the points that matter is not run; the census limits 2cos(jπ/n)/m in 8.8 point that way.
  6. Lean. C1 and C2 are algebra in c = cosh, s = sinh and would sit beside J4 in BallJoins. Not written.
