## Building the ball from a directed step

I want to know what can be kept when the description of a system changes. The ball work gives that question an experimental meaning. Give each face a clock, connect two faces, and ask what reaches the next cell, what remains in the gap, and what survives a reversal. In my physical picture the nucleus is the centre, the cloud supplies the wall, and small electron balls form the ground between distinct walls. One ball breathing out and its neighbour taking up the disturbance is the mechanism I propose. Two balls pushed together connect at certain levels but always leave a layer of gap, and I read that gap as the spacing, angles and connections of the crystal lattice. Light leads, the wall acts as a critical angle, and the forced gap is what I call my Snell's law at the hypersurface and Pauli exclusion. In the executable models, a clock, a port, a transmitted amplitude and a detector reading are four different objects. Keeping their names attached to their equations is how I find out which parts of the picture the experiments actually test.

**DERIVED.** An assigned positive clock ratio $z$ gives three coordinates,
$$r = \log z,\qquad a = \tanh(r/4),\qquad \Gamma = \tanh(r/2) = \frac{2a}{1+a^2}.$$
The inverse is $z = [(1+a)/(1-a)]^2$. A face apex and an interface reflection both recover the same clock, by different maps. The example $z = 3$ has $a = 2-\sqrt3$ and $\Gamma = 1/2$. Four aligned directed steps of this size have total logarithmic increment $4\log 3$, equivalent ratio 81 and equivalent reflection $40/41$. That calculation prescribes a sequence of clocks. A wave at a specified frequency crossing four finite gaps also gathers phases, and predicting the transmitted intensity needs those phases and the boundary conditions. A length written as $\log z$ is dimensionless until a length scale is supplied.

### The face apex has an exact one-electron meaning

The quarter-logarithm in the apex turns out to be exactly the coordinate of a textbook atomic process. Take a sudden change between two same-centre Coulomb Hamiltonians with the same mass, and let $\alpha$ and $\beta$ be their inverse length scales. For a fixed orbital label the binding-energy ratio is $z = (\beta/\alpha)^2$, and
$$a = \tanh\!\Big(\frac{\ln z}{4}\Big) = \frac{\beta-\alpha}{\beta+\alpha}.$$
Put $t = 1-a^2$. Direct orbital integration gives the squared overlaps — the probabilities of finding the electron still in the same-label orbital after the change —
$$P_{1s} = t^3,\qquad P_{2s} = t^3\,(1-3a^2)^2,\qquad P_{2p} = t^5 .$$
For 1s the overlap is $8(\alpha\beta)^{3/2}/(\alpha+\beta)^3$, whose square is exactly $(1-a^2)^3$. At charge doubling, $z = 4$, the three probabilities are $512/729 = 70.23\%$, $2048/6561 = 31.21\%$ and $32768/59049 = 55.49\%$. At the wall itself, $a = \sqrt2-1$, each face keeps $t = 2(\sqrt2-1) \approx 0.828$, and
$$P_{1s} = 8(\sqrt2-1)^3 = 8(5\sqrt2-7)\approx 56.85\%,\qquad P_{2s}\approx13.39\%,\qquad P_{2p}\approx39.02\% .$$
Equal energy ratios can therefore put survival on opposite sides of one half depending on the orbital. Summing over all bound final states answers a different question. **DERIVED**, checked by independent quadrature (70 quadratures in the orbital folder, and a separate integration that reproduces every digit above).

The sudden-change overlap itself is established atomic physics — it is the same formula used for an electron staying bound when a nucleus changes charge in beta decay. What is new is that its scale coordinate is my face apex. In this derivation $z$ compares two one-electron Hamiltonians; carrying it to two successive measured removals in one many-electron atom needs the initial state, the final projector and the physical change specified. That is a structural link, not yet a measurement.

A three-state loop of orbital projectors at scales 1, 2 and 4 carries a sign: its three-state trace is negative for 2s and positive for 2p. The orbital label, not only the energy ratio, decides the turn of the loop. **DERIVED** (Coulomb loop folder).

**Hypothesis and its control.** I assign clocks to the layers inside an atom and compare the facing clocks of its neighbours. Swapping them changes the experiment. At a matched junction $z = 1$ and $\Gamma = 0$ even when the internal ladder is steep. For sodium's L opening, the weakest projector link changes from about 0.000292 to 0.000865 when the opening moves from the facing link into the cell. For argon's first ordinary step the change is about 0.000804 to 0.000874. The difference between these two outputs drops by about 98 percent, and both stay below $1/12$. Both runs use two-level hydrogen cells. The result isolates the effect of assigning clocks to different links; it supplies no conductivity for either element.

### Six experiments that give "wall", "gap" and "tree" different jobs

**1. Balls in the sea.** The research folder implements three choices of state preparation and dynamics. Model A builds a global Hamiltonian with site and link terms. Model B starts with occupied cells and empty links. Model C releases a disturbance from an occupied resting state with finite travel on the connections. The 396 recorded executions span 100 settings, or "ways". A successful execution only says the program returned. A representative sealed A run conserves its ledger and yet responds across the model too early for the proposed transit rule. C can pass the transit test while missing the prescribed threshold for an initial non-local block. Preparing a correlated ground state and sending a new disturbance through it ask different questions. Changing a numerical detection threshold changes what counts as a first arrival in a continuous-time lattice. The squeeze test modulates a clock sinusoidally; associating its stored energy with a thermodynamic temperature needs an independently specified ensemble or thermometer.

**2. Hydrogen in the space between.** Each centre has six facing ports. Gap sites carry hoppings $c = c_0\,\mathrm{sech}\big(\tfrac12\log z\big)$. Let $P$ be the occupied one-particle projector and $C_i = P_{ii}$ its restriction to a cell. The $ii$ block of $P^2 = P$ gives
$$C_i^2 + \sum_{j\ne i}P_{ij}P_{ji} = C_i,\qquad \sum_{j\ne i}\|P_{ij}\|_F^2 = \operatorname{Tr}\big(C_i - C_i^2\big).$$
The trace is the available correlation weight; the individual squared block norms say where that weight goes. A two-centre, separation-four, half-filled example has 75 sites and 150 one-particle coordinates. Its weakest path link is about $6.416\times10^{-4}$ against the study's $1/12$ marker. The opposed setting has lower energy than the parallel setting by about 0.09968 model energy units at this fixed separation. Other separations change the number of sites, so total energies there need a common reference before a binding curve can be inferred. Threshold first arrivals in the recorded sweep occur at 0.7, 3.9 and 8.1 model time units at separations two, four and six. *Test:* hold the Hilbert space, filling and energy reference fixed and compare the force inferred from the separation derivative; freeze the detector threshold before the travel test and publish the threshold dependence.

**3. Hydrogen and the light between.** Timing enters explicitly here. In the recorded aligned and detuned runs the first detection at C moves from 5.37 to 8.67, while the light at C moves from 0.0250 to 0.0161. Those values depend on a detector and an update rule. One original regression criterion asked the zero-coupling model to reproduce v4 to $10^{-12}$; a later comparison records a discrepancy of 0.0743 at B and withdraws that criterion because the face positions differ. Both the discrepancy and its reason belong in the record. *Test:* match the geometries and input pulse before attributing any residual to a new propagation law.

**4. Seven balls, my light.** A central cell and six neighbours separate the intrinsic wall-ray experiment from light propagated through the room. The intrinsic rule keeps a ray near radius 0.985 in a representative run; the room rule can carry light into other balls. One finite trace accounts for 12 units emitted as about 2.6635 in flight, 9.3364 in the ground and 0.0001 below the cut. These are entries in a code ledger. The proposed links to optical transmission, absorption and heating each need a map from such entries to an instrument.

**5. The ball is the tree.** The 37-cell construction has thirty leaves. Comparing paths by summed intrinsic edge length gives two layers of total length $2\log3$ in the example. The arrival test respects the layer delay, and a leaf's ground does not transmit onward under the specified rule. The proposed equality of a pair of twins fails its initial tolerance, with a difference near $7\times10^{-5}$. Refining the time step from 0.04 to 0.005 reduces another twin comparison from $4.70\times10^{-4}$ to $2.38\times10^{-5}$; a convergence trend leaves the exact equality open. The model gives an operational meaning to my phrase "it just knows layers". Equal total layer depth need not mean equal local environments, sources or path histories.

**6. Two atoms in a ball.** The container adds a separate wall-work term. Across eight settings, the total of energy in cells and stretches plus outgoing energy, less supplied wall work, differs from the unit input by at most about $1.6\times10^{-14}$. A single moving-mirror control with reflectivity amplitude $\rho = 1/2$ and dimensionless speed $v = 0.2$ has reflected energy $\rho^2(1+v)/(1-v) = 0.375$ and supplied work 0.125. The strict zero-outside control for a closed container misses zero by about $7.884\times10^{-5}$ in one run and $2.59\times10^{-5}$ after refinement. Conservation and confinement are independent tests, and treating the small residual as exactly zero would erase one of them.

### What a projector link can read

**DERIVED with conditions.** Put $B_i = \operatorname{Tr}(C_i - C_i^2)$ and specify $n$ equivalent candidate neighbours. The proposed link marker is $X_{ij} > B_i/(3n)$. One half-filled mode gives $B_i = 1/4$; two equivalent neighbours would give $1/24$, and four give $1/48$. A number reported for a particular lattice also depends on the chosen mode count, coordination and projector. The unnormalised $1/12$ arises as $(1/4)/3$. The sum rule licenses an allocation of weight; the factor of three and the choice of neighbour shell need their own model assumptions. In the follow-up runs a half-filled bcc band passes, sufficiently dilute fillings fail (the proved sufficient bound is $f\le1/25$), and a filled gapped band passes. The gapped positive is decisive for interpretation: the criterion can select concentrated intercell weights in an insulator. A conductivity claim needs a current operator, a response protocol, and a thermodynamic or stated finite-size limit. The Bruggeman one-third marker belongs to a separately specified effective-medium comparison. A chemical element carries no universal "above one third" label independent of phase and observable.

The same sum rule is already known in quantum chemistry: the sum of delocalisation indices of an atom over its partners equals its own fluctuation, and the localisation tensor is the second moment of the same weights. My junction budget and that identity are one fact, read on my projector. **DERIVED.**

### Two unequal faces, one exact wall

For the specified two-boost commutator the trace is $2 - 4\sinh^2(h_a)\sinh^2(h_b)$, where $h_i = \tfrac12\log z_i$. At trace $-2$ the product of the squared hyperbolic sines is one. With $u = \Gamma_a^2$, $v = \Gamma_b^2$ and $u, v<1$, the condition $uv = (1-u)(1-v)$ reduces exactly to $u+v = 1$. Ordinary forward steps allow positive square roots; keeping the squares keeps both reverse branches. Equal faces give $u = v = 1/2$. At the boundary the usual Jordan test decides whether a matrix of trace $-2$ is a non-trivial parabolic block or the scalar $-I$.

In my convention carbon's helium-core opening is on the open side with both faces the same, and nitrogen's is on the closed side with both faces the same. The mixed pair — carbon's helium-core opening ratio paired with nitrogen's — is barely open: $\Gamma_a^2 + \Gamma_b^2 - 1 = +0.00309$. Among the 427 adjacent same-core pairs of first openings, it is the pair nearest ideal contact: 5 pairs open and 422 close, and this pair's margin is below 0.0031 against rivals above 0.037. Holding carbon at $392.09056/64.49352 = 6.079534$, the partner's forward ratio for exact contact is $5.591156$. The five open pairs are lithium with beryllium, beryllium with boron, boron with carbon, carbon with nitrogen and sodium with magnesium, all helium-core or neon-core first openings. In the angle form, with $\theta = \arcsin\Gamma$, the loop test is $\theta_a + \theta_b < 90^\circ$; the boundary pair of each core — the last element outside paired with the first inside — reads carbon with nitrogen $+0.18^\circ$ (open), sodium with magnesium $+6.67^\circ$ (open), potassium with calcium $-2.21^\circ$, rubidium with strontium $-6.52^\circ$ and caesium with barium $-10.29^\circ$ (all close). Allowing the two faces to meet at an angle other than a right angle, the carbon–nitrogen loop closes only for meeting angles between $13.115^\circ$ and $89.823^\circ$. These claims concern the selected commutator with clocks read from two removal energies. Demonstrating a physical closed path would need an independently measured return observable. The unequal-face test is more informative than labelling two elements, because fixing one face predicts quantitatively where the partner crosses.
