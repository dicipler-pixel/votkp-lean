# A fixed-threshold family with continuously variable transition weight

Jeromie Beasley's ordered peel records ratios of energy thresholds. Can selecting an independent line-strength observable make the elementary geometry itself determine the outcome? A small model establishes what additional input such a claim needs.

Consider a fixed initial state |g> and two orthogonal accessible channels |1>,|2>, with positive photon energies E₁,E₂. Keep the spectrum, its entire ordered list, the equilibrium populations and the density of final channels fixed. Take one polarization sector of a Hermitian probe D. Set the transition matrix elements

    <1|D|g> = sqrt(H t/E₁),   <2|D|g> = sqrt(H(1−t)/E₂),   0≤t≤1, H>0.

Include their Hermitian conjugates with all other couplings in this reduced example fixed. In weak linear response the two energy-weighted strengths E_j |<j|D|g>|² are Ht and H(1−t). Their sum stays H. The first share moves continuously from zero to one with exactly the same thresholds and the same integrated two-channel weight. This works even if both strengths and both energies are strictly positive. The settings t=1/4 and t=3/4 reverse the ordering. At the spectral level the states are the same throughout; only the transition operator changes. Equivalently one may specify the same external readout in laboratory coordinates and rotate a compatible eigenbasis across two models sharing the spectrum. If symmetry distinguishes the two channels, that latter rotation would require a separate justification.

This is an identifiability control. It demonstrates that the threshold list plus a positive conserved two-channel strength does not mathematically force a line-yield ordering. It is not a complete atomic oscillator-strength sum rule or an ab initio calculation for carbon or oxygen. Applying a specified spherical-tensor operator, spin and angular-momentum rules, radial wavefunctions, preparation alignment, polarization and detector acceptance can constrain those couplings. An IQGT/projector account would have to identify the occupied or channel projectors and the actual perturbation along which they are interrogated. A proposed prediction will be appreciably stronger if it specifies those data and includes a prediction for the fixed-endpoint null. The two-parameter energy table by itself leaves them free.

For comparison, a particular microscopic dynamics *can* determine a weight. Under a sudden one-electron Coulomb quench with unchanged reduced mass, a normalized 1s orbital is ψₐ=(a³/π)¹ᐟ² exp(−ar). Its ground-to-ground overlap is 8(ab)³ᐟ²/(a+b)³ using ∫₀^∞ r² exp(−sr)dr=2/s³. With u=b/a, the survival probability is [4u/(1+u)²]³=[1−Γ(u)²]³. The binding-energy ratio is z=u². The model explicitly identifies the half-step Γ(√z), and fixes the cube because the wavefunctions and overlap integral were specified. For u=2 the result is 512/729, a theoretical value in the ideal sudden atomic tritium example. At the peel-energy setting z=35/6 this survival observable and the real-port reflectance answer different experimental questions. Conventional calculations of finite-speed final-state corrections are documented by [Williams and Koonin, 1983](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevC.27.1815). Neither the microscopic quench nor the port calibration models a many-electron carbon ionization channel.

In the element table it is legitimate to record an independently sourced oscillator strength next to a peel. To test the geometric wall one must specify which function of that observable should change and why, prior to inspecting that outcome.
