# A direct half-power test of the equal-face setting

This test makes the two sides of the geometric wall observable with one passive resistive termination. It is an established transmission-line comparison. Agreement calibrates one physical realization of Jeromie Beasley's reflection variable. Identifying an atomic ionization-energy ratio with a physical wave impedance takes an additional coupling law.

## Input and output are measured separately

Use a real reference impedance R₀ and normalize the load Z/R₀=x+iy. The voltage reflection coefficient is (Z−R₀)/(Z+R₀). For a lossless single line with a passive termination, the reflected and accepted fractions of incident power are

    R = [(x−1)²+y²]/[(x+1)²+y²],
    A = 4x/[(x+1)²+y²].

With no other energy path A is dissipated in the load. In the real case z=x>1, R>A precisely when z>3+2√2. For z=35/6, R=841/1681 and A=840/1681. The excess is 1/1681 of the incident power (about 0.05949%). The geometric cutoff predicts more reflected power. A rule assigning the transition to the Ag–Si midpoint would put this same setting on the other side **if that rule is proposed for this same apparatus and observable**. A rival proposed only for atomic data makes no such microwave prediction.

A nominal 300 Ω resistor in parallel with 10,500 Ω gives 291⅔ Ω. With a nominal 50 Ω reference, z=35/6. Component labels and DC measurements are insufficient to establish the realized AC value. Calibrate the complex impedance at the operating frequency and reference plane by an independent bridge or equivalent metrology. Blind the output analyst to load settings. Record incident and reflected power independently of the input-impedance calculation. A separate dissipated-power measurement can reveal parasitic loss. Use reflection standards, common reference planes, randomized load order and insertion-loss checks. All stated powers are ratios of measurements normalized to incident power. [NIST procedures for directional measurements and mismatch](https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/calibrations/im-17-4.pdf)

Equal-arm controls z=1, 29/5, 35/6, 169/29, 204/35 cover matched, below and above the wall. If independently measured R and A have a common absolute bound ε, the difference has worst-case bound 2ε, so ε<1/3362 suffices to resolve the ideal 35/6 difference in the absence of other errors. If measuring R alone and using independently validated flux balance, R must be resolved relative to 1/2 at that same tolerance. State calibration errors and propagate their covariance. If the measured error interval includes zero, classify unresolved.

## An experimentally relevant failure mode

Reactance moves the boundary to the circle (x−3)²+y²=8. For x=5.82, switching normalized reactance y from 0 to 1/4 takes the load from below to above the half-power boundary with the real part unchanged. The exact tangent example x=5,y=2 is on the circle. A scalar model applied to a reactive cell without certification can give the wrong answer. This complex-plane circle is a statement about this one laboratory wave port. The intrinsic element record has its own stated domain. The accompanying Lean file proves flux balance, the complex circle, both controls and the calibration result from exact rational definitions. No physical instrument was run during this work.

## Why an energy ratio alone does not determine the flux

As a false mapping control, match a wavefunction and its derivative at a one-dimensional sharp step with equal masses and two positive kinetic energies. The continuity equations are 1+r=t and k₁(1−r)=k₂t. Hence r=(k₁−k₂)/(k₁+k₂), where k is proportional to the square root of the kinetic energy. For E₁/E₂=35/6 this gives reflected fraction 0.17171726. The resistor interpretation of the same input number gave 0.50029744. This elementary counterexample uses kinetic energies in a step model; the atomic peel uses successive many-electron removal thresholds. Associating a particular measured flux with an elemental ratio requires the named preparation and boundary conditions that justify that identification. Matching the algebraic form of Γ alone leaves even the exponent of the ratio undetermined.
